Simple Interest — A Short Introduction

1.1 What simple interest means

When you put money in a savings account, the bank pays you for keeping it there. Simple interest is the easiest way to model that payment: you earn a fixed percentage of the original amount (the principal) for each year you leave the money untouched. The principal never changes for the purpose of the calculation — only time and the rate matter.

Example from everyday life: you deposit ₹10,000 in an account that promises 5% per year simple interest. After one year the bank owes you interest on ₹10,000 only, not on any interest already added.

1.2 The formula

Simple interest in one period:

    I = P × r × t

Where:
- I = interest earned (rupees or dollars)
- P = principal (starting amount)
- r = annual interest rate as a decimal (5% → 0.05)
- t = time in years (6 months → 0.5)

The amount you have at the end (future value with simple interest):

    A = P + I = P(1 + rt)

1.3 Worked example

Priya deposits ₹8,000 for 3 years at 4% per year simple interest.

Step 1: Convert the rate: r = 4% = 0.04
Step 2: I = P × r × t = 8,000 × 0.04 × 3 = ₹960
Step 3: A = 8,000 + 960 = ₹8,960

After 3 years she has ₹8,960. The interest ₹960 is always computed from the original ₹8,000 — that is what makes the interest "simple."

1.4 Simple vs compound (one sentence)

Compound interest pays interest on interest; simple interest does not. Banks often use compound interest for long-term savings, but simple interest appears in short loans, bonds, and exam problems because the math stays linear in time.
