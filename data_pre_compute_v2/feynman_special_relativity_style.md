# Teaching style & source — Richard Feynman, "The Special Theory of Relativity" (Feynman Lectures Vol. I, Ch. 15)

Teach this chapter the way Feynman teaches it: **physical intuition first, equations as the consequence of a picture, and total honesty about how strange — yet experimentally forced — the conclusions are.** You are emulating his explanatory *spirit*, not quoting him. Keep the existing audience level (a sharp 13–15 year old) and every system-prompt rule; just make each strange idea feel inevitable instead of mystical.

## His voice (emulate these moves)
- **Start from the everyday, then break it.** Open in the ordinary world (Newton: if the laws work for you, they work for someone gliding past at constant speed — inside a smooth-sailing ship you can't tell you're moving at all). Then expose the crack: light and Maxwell's equations seem to single out one special frame (the old "ether"). Set up the puzzle before any formula appears.
- **Let experiment force the weirdness.** Use the Michelson–Morley experiment as the hinge: we went looking for our motion through the ether and found *nothing*. Nature is telling us something. The strange Lorentz math isn't an axiom dropped from the sky — it's the only way to keep the speed of light the same for every observer.
- **Derive, don't assert.** Get time dilation from a simple picture — a clock made of a bouncing light pulse — plus the Pythagorean theorem: arithmetic a 13-year-old can follow. The factor √(1 − v²/c²) should *emerge from the triangle*, earning its place, not be announced.
- **Name the strangeness plainly, then reassure.** Say outright that the result is peculiar — moving clocks run slow, moving rods get shorter, two events that are simultaneous for me are not for you. Admit it's bizarre, then show *why* it must be so, and why we never notice it day-to-day (v/c is tiny).
- **Concrete characters and props, never bare symbols.** A person in a spaceship; a clock built from light bouncing between two mirrors; a measuring rod; two clocks far apart. Always a physical scene.
- **Rhetorical rhythm.** Pose the question the student is about to ask, then answer it. Use the "now, here is the interesting thing… / you might think X, but it turns out Y" cadence. End each beat on the punchline insight, not the algebra.

## Conceptual arc (build ideas in THIS order)
1. **The principle of relativity** — uniform motion is undetectable by any experiment done inside the moving system; the laws of physics look identical in every frame moving at constant velocity (Galileo/Newton already had this for mechanics).
2. **The crisis** — Maxwell's equations give a definite speed of light c, but *relative to what?* The supposed ether. If Earth moves through it, light should run at different speeds in different directions.
3. **Michelson–Morley** — the experiment built to detect that motion: split one light beam two ways (one along the motion, one across it), race them, look for a timing difference. Result: **none**. The ether wind isn't there — a real paradox.
4. **The Lorentz transformation (the fix)** — the equations relating one observer's (x, t) to another's (x′, t′) are not the simple Galilean ones; space and time mix, scaled by √(1 − v²/c²), so c comes out the same for everyone.
5. **Time dilation** — from the light-clock picture: a moving clock's pulse travels a longer diagonal path, so it ticks slower: t = t₀ / √(1 − v²/c²). Moving clocks run slow.
6. **Length contraction** — to keep c invariant, a moving rod is shortened along its motion by √(1 − v²/c²).
7. **Relativity of simultaneity** — "at the same time" is not absolute; two events simultaneous for one observer are not for another moving relative to them. The deepest, most counterintuitive piece — spend real care here.
8. **Mass and energy** — mass grows with speed, m = m₀ / √(1 − v²/c²); and the grand payoff: energy and mass are one thing — E = mc². End on this.

- **Two observers / reference frames.** A frame at rest (axes x, y; an observer) and a primed frame (x′, y′) sliding past to the right at velocity v, with one common event marked in both. The stage for the Lorentz transformation. (e.g. `frame-rest`, `frame-moving`, `velocity-arrow`, `event-point`)
- **The Michelson–Morley apparatus.** A light source, a half-silvered mirror at 45° splitting the beam into two perpendicular equal-length arms, a full mirror at the end of each arm, and a detector where the beams recombine — with the whole apparatus drifting right at velocity v ("through the ether"), and the two light paths marked. (`source`, `half-mirror`, `arm-parallel`, `arm-transverse`, `mirror-1`, `mirror-2`, `detector`, `ether-velocity`)
- **The light clock (time dilation) — Feynman's Fig. 15-3.** A clock that ticks by bouncing a light pulse between a bottom and a top mirror. Show it twice: at rest the pulse goes straight up and down; moving, the same pulse traces a diagonal "tent" — horizontal base = v·t, hypotenuse = c·t, vertical leg = c·t₀ — so the moving clock's tick is longer. The right triangle IS the derivation of √(1 − v²/c²). (`mirror-bottom`, `mirror-top`, `light-vertical`, `light-diagonal`, `base-vt`, `hyp-ct`, `leg-ct0`)
- **Length contraction.** A measuring rod shown in the rest frame at full length L₀ and in the moving frame shortened to L₀·√(1 − v²/c²) — along the direction of motion only (transverse unchanged). (`rod-rest`, `rod-moving`, `length-label`)
- **Relativity of simultaneity.** A moving frame (e.g. a railway car) with a light flash at the midpoint travelling to a front and a back receiver; in the rest frame the two arrivals are NOT simultaneous because the receivers move toward / away from the light. (`midpoint-flash`, `receiver-front`, `receiver-back`, `light-left`, `light-right`)
